Okonkwo, Lindqvist & TanakaIntrinsic dimension of learned representations

3Geometry of the representation

Let $h(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the activation of the penultimate layer for an input $x$, with $d = 4096$ throughout. We are interested not in the span of these vectors but in the shape of the set they occupy. Following Facco et al., we estimate the intrinsic dimension from the ratio of each point's second- and first-nearest-neighbour distances,

$$\hat{k} = \left( \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log \frac{r_2(x_i)}{r_1(x_i)} \right)^{-1}$$(1)

which uses only the two closest points to each sample. We hypothesise that the representation lies on a low-dimensional manifold embedded in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and that the intrinsic dimension $k \ll d$ is stable across the later layers. Figure 2 supports the second half of this claim: from layer 18 onward the estimate settles between 22 and 26 while $d$ is unchanged.

Figure 2. Schematic of the manifold picture. Activations (dots) are sampled near a curved surface of dimension $k$ inside the ambient space of dimension $d$. The surface itself is not linear, so a flat subspace of dimension $k$ does not contain it.

A linear baseline is instructive. PCA retaining 95% of the variance overestimates $k$ by a factor of three to six on every model we tested (Table 2), which we take as evidence that the manifold is curved rather than a subspace. The gap widens with depth.

To discourage the network from expanding the surface during fine-tuning we add a penalty on the local expansion of the map, the trace of $J_\theta(x)^{\top} J_\theta(x)$, where $J_\theta$ is the Jacobian of $h$ with respect to its input:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[ \ell\big(f_\theta(x), y\big) \right] + \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[ \operatorname{tr}\!\big( J_\theta(x)^{\top} J_\theta(x) \big) \right]$$(2)

With $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ the estimated dimension of the fine-tuned model moves by less than one across all layers, while accuracy is unchanged to within noise. We return to the choice of $\lambda$ in Section 5.

It is a claim about the activations, not the raw inputs. Take any input $x$ and read off the network's hidden vector $h(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, with $d = 4096$ here p.4. Across the dataset those vectors do not fill the 4096-dimensional space. They sit near a curved surface that can be described with far fewer coordinates:

$$h(x) \approx \phi(z), \qquad z \in \mathbb{R}^k,\; k \ll d$$

How is this estimated in practice? Why the ratio of two neighbours and not, say, a local PCA?
Claude CodeOpus 5 · 10:19

Eq. 1 is the TwoNN estimator p.4. For each point $x_i$ take the distances to its nearest and second-nearest neighbours, $r_1$ and $r_2$, and form the ratio $\mu_i = r_2 / r_1$.

The useful fact is that if points are sampled from a $k$-dimensional surface, $\mu$ follows a Pareto distribution whose shape parameter is exactly $k$, whatever the surface's curvature:

$$P(\mu > t) = t^{-k}, \qquad t \ge 1$$

So $k$ can be read off the tail of the observed ratios. The paper uses the closed-form maximum-likelihood version:

$$\hat{k} = \left( \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log \frac{r_2(x_i)}{r_1(x_i)} \right)^{-1}$$

Why two neighbours and not a local PCA? Using only the two closest points keeps the estimate local, so curvature at larger scales does not leak in, and no basis is fitted at all. A local PCA needs a neighbourhood size and a variance threshold, and both push the answer upward on a curved surface. That is what lets TwoNN disagree with PCA p.9.

One caveat the paper raises: the estimate drifts upward when $m$ is small, so they report it at $m = 20{,}000$ and check stability by subsampling p.9.
